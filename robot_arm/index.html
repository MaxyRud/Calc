Moving the finger motors to the body saves less than you'd think. They already sit at the elbow end of the forearm, close to the joint, so they add little leverage.

The four rules behind it

  1. Heavy things close to the body. Every gram near the hand costs about four times as much shoulder torque as the same gram near the elbow.
  2. Tendons cross as few joints as possible. Here they only cross the wrist. Each extra joint adds friction, stretch, and coupling (the fingers moving on their own when the arm moves).
  3. Tendons go through the middle of the joint they cross. The tendons run up a hollow centre post and through the open ring of the U-joint. A string through a joint's pivot doesn't get longer or shorter when the joint bends, so bending the wrist doesn't pull the fingers. Your wrist works the same way: the finger tendons pass through the carpal tunnel.
  4. Twist goes on the elbow side of the finger motors. Because the twist joint is before them, the finger motors turn together with the hand and the tendons never wind up.

Your own arm follows the same plan: the big muscles are in the shoulder and upper arm, and most finger muscles are in the forearm with long tendons into the hand.

If the forearm gets too heavy

Move just one strong, slow channel (a power grip that closes all fingers together) to the body on a Bowden cable. Keep the 16 fast, precise channels in the forearm.

What this page doesn't do yet

The wrist module is the checked piston wrist, so its reach and clearances are verified. The rest of the arm is a layout study: the sizes are realistic, but the shoulder and elbow parts aren't clearance-checked, and the wrist moves at L16 speed (about 58°/s).
