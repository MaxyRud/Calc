The idea

  1. Two hinges. The hand sits on a universal joint at the top of the centre post. One hinge bends the hand towards the palm or back. The other, at right angles, tips it to the thumb or little-finger side. The joint carries the hand's weight, so the actuators only have to steer.
  2. Four pistons on the diagonals. Each L16 linear actuator runs from the cup floor up to a corner of the hand plate: palm-thumb, palm-little, back-thumb and back-little. To bend towards the palm, both palm-side pistons pull in and both back-side ones push out. To tip towards the thumb, both thumb-side ones pull in. Every move uses all four, like pairs of muscles where one side pulls while the other lets go. Mixing the two gives every diagonal and full circles.
  3. Why diagonals. With one piston per side, the piston on the outside of a big bend has to swing its rod right over the top of the hinge, and that stops it at about 65°. On the diagonals, the rods pass beside the hinge, so the wrist bends past 80°. You also get about 40% more torque.
  4. Twist. The whole cup, with pistons, post and hand, turns on a thin bearing. A servo under it does the forearm twist, palm up or down.

Piston lengths

Li = | R(bend, side) · ti − bi |
R = Rz(side) · Rx(−bend)
ti: top joint, 28 mm out on a diagonal, level with the hinge
bi: bottom joint, 31 mm out, 150 mm below

The page and the Arduino code both solve this for every pose. At rest each actuator is 150.0 mm pin to pin, which is 23.5 mm of its 50 mm stroke out. That leaves almost equal travel both ways.

What stops it

Straight bend or side: at about 82°, the front and back actuators on one side close in on each other. On a diagonal, the stroke runs out first, at 66–71°. That is still about twice what a human wrist can do on a diagonal. Every pose is checked for stroke, both piston parts against the post, rods against the U-joint, yokes and hand plate, and the pistons against each other. The motion stops before anything touches. The post narrows to a 9 mm neck so the inner rods clear it on deep bends.

The top joints swing up to about 86° from the rod, so they have to be small forks with a two-pin cross. A ball link only tilts about 30°. The bottom joints never tilt more than about 9°, so plain M3 ball links are fine there.

Robot vs human

MotionHumanThis wrist

Human range is the usual clinical full range. Most everyday tasks need much less: about 54° flexion, 60° extension, 17° radial and 40° ulnar (Ryu et al. 1991). Sources: wrist ROM review, normal ROM table, functional ROM (Mayo), ROMs used in robot wrists.

Honest trade-offs

  1. Slower than a real wrist. L16 actuators move 20 mm/s (63:1) or 8 mm/s (150:1). That is roughly 58°/s or 23°/s at the wrist. The "Real" speed setting shows this.
  2. Four actuators for two angles. All four have to agree exactly, or they push against each other and draw stall current. Set a trim per actuator so all four read the same length at rest (it's in the code).
  3. Holds without power. The lead screws don't back-drive, so the hand stays put when the power is off.
  4. Check the real drawing. The clearances assume a 17 mm actuator body and 6 mm rod. Check them against the Actuonix drawing before you print.

Arduino sketch · Nano, Servo library

Send three numbers over serial at 115200 baud: bend, side and twist in degrees, for example 40 -15 0. Send c to draw a circle. The sketch eases between poses at the actuator speed, so the four pistons stay in step.
